A Brief History of Energy

From deep-sea vents to the solar panel on your roof: four billion years of borrowing power from the sun, and why the carbon matters.

A light, eight-minute tour of energy on Earth: vents, sunlight, fire, coal, war, oil, atoms, carbon and sunshine again.

It was a normal Monday lunchtime. Then, at 12:33, the lights went out across the whole Iberian Peninsula. Trains stopped between stations, traffic lights went blank and phones slowly ran out of battery. For half a day, millions of us remembered something we usually forget:

almost everything we do runs on energy that comes from somewhere else.

So where does it come from? To answer that properly, we need to go back a long way. Much further than the first light bulb, or even the first campfire. Grab a coffee (itself a little parcel of stored sunshine) and let’s start at the bottom of the ocean.

01 Deep time

The first spark was in the dark

The story of energy on Earth may begin somewhere the sun never reaches. Around four billion years ago, at the bottom of the ocean, hydrothermal vents (underwater geysers) were pumping out scalding water full of chemicals. Many scientists think this is where life got going, with tiny microbes making their food from those chemicals instead of from light. That trick is called chemosynthesis, and creatures still do it at vents today.

Up on the surface, the sun was already shining, but nothing could use it yet. That took time. Somewhere around 3 billion years ago, tiny ocean microbes called cyanobacteria worked out photosynthesis: using sunlight to turn water and carbon dioxide into food, and releasing oxygen as waste.

It was like an explosion of energy. Around 2.4 billion years ago, their oxygen began to fill the air, completely rewriting the planet’s chemistry, climate and biology. Fun fact: every breath you take is thanks to those tiny microbes, the planet’s first solar panels.

Plants carried the trick onto land much later. And from then on, for hundreds of millions of years, almost every animal on Earth, from dinosaurs to early mammals, was powered by sunlight stored in plants, either by eating them or by eating something that did.

02 Fire

Fire, mills and a happy accident

Then, around a million years ago, our ancestors learned to control fire. (The oldest solid evidence is ash and burnt bone in Wonderwerk Cave, South Africa.) It was a new way to release energy, but the fuel was the same as ever: wood and plants grown by the sun.

Fire meant warmth, light, safety from predators and, crucially, cooking. Cooked food is softer and easier to digest, so we get more energy out of every bite. Some scientists, like Harvard’s Richard Wrangham, think this helped shrink our jaws and teeth and grow our brains. In a way, cooking helped make us human.

As we got more inventive, we learned to catch moving water and wind. Waterwheels ground grain, and by the 9th century windmills were turning in Persia.

Around the same time, Chinese alchemists were searching for a potion for eternal life. They mixed saltpetre, sulphur and charcoal, and got the opposite: gunpowder, which they called “fire medicine”. It would go on to ignite an arms race around the world.

Notice something? All of this still ran on today’s sunshine. It was renewable but limited: you could only burn as much wood as your forests could grow.

03 Coal

Buried sunshine and the age of steam

The way out of that limit was coal: a black rock that burns hotter and longer than wood. People had burned it for centuries. By the 11th century, China’s iron industry had already used up so many trees that it switched from charcoal to coal. But it was in Britain in the 1700s that things really took off.

Britain had plenty of coal but a big problem: the deeper the mines went, the more they flooded. In 1712, Thomas Newcomen installed a steam engine near Dudley to pump the water out. Think about that: a machine burning coal to help dig out more coal.

Then a Scottish instrument maker called James Watt added a separate condenser (patented in 1769) that made steam engines far more efficient. Suddenly engines could leave the mines. Canals, railways and factories sprang up across the country to move and burn the huge amounts of coal needed for machines, iron and new weapons.

Coal went from about 2% of the world’s energy in 1800 to roughly half by 1900. And every time you read “W” on a kettle, that’s the watt, named after Watt.

04 Smoke & war

Smoke, steel and the thirst for oil

Coal was powering industry worldwide, but it was far from clean. People noticed the pollution almost immediately, and as industry grew it became a deadly crisis: darkened skies, blackened buildings, damaged plants and wildlife, and sick lungs.

The worst moment came in December 1952, when a cold, still fog trapped coal smoke over London for five days. People couldn’t see their own feet. The Great Smog killed around 4,000 people at the time, and later estimates go as high as 12,000. It led to the Clean Air Act of 1956, and coal-burning power stations were gradually moved out of big cities.

But we couldn’t curb our demand for energy. Quite the opposite: two world wars pushed it higher than ever.

Militaries needed more concentrated energy to move massive machines. Oil packs more punch than coal and is easier to load, so in 1912, under Winston Churchill, Britain’s navy began switching its battleships from coal to oil. By WW1, oil was fuelling tanks, ships and the first warplanes.

WW2 pushed fuels to their limits. Fighter planes ran on high-octane aviation fuel, and explosives were made from nitrogen compounds. Germany’s V-2 rocket burned alcohol and liquid oxygen, and that alcohol was fermented from crops (reportedly lots of potatoes). So that one ran on today’s sunshine! Nearly everything else ran on ancient sunlight, trapped in plants millions of years ago.

We never really swapped one energy source for another.
We just kept adding.

05 Atoms & carbon

Wires, atoms and a curve that keeps rising

By now, humans were getting very creative with energy. Oil moved our cars, ships and planes, and became the plastics in almost everything we own. Since Edison’s first power station in 1882, electricity had been flowing down wires into homes. And in 1942 we learned to split the atom: by 1954, the first nuclear power plant was feeding a grid, with no fossil fuel needed.

We also got very good at measuring things. In 1958, a scientist called Charles Keeling started recording the CO₂ in the air on top of Mauna Loa, a volcano in Hawaii. His line, the Keeling Curve, has gone up every year since: from about 316 parts per million in 1959 to about 427 in 2025. And how about before anyone was measuring? Air bubbles trapped in ancient ice suggest it was roughly 280 before the Industrial Revolution.

Scientists realised that the extra CO₂ was trapping heat, raising temperatures and melting ice, with serious effects for every habitat on the planet. And they realised we were causing it, by burning coal, oil, gas and wood.

Here’s the twist. Around 300 million years ago, in a period called the Carboniferous (literally “coal-bearing”), vast swamp forests soaked up CO₂, died, and got buried before they could rot. Over millions of years, the heat and pressure turned them into coal.

That’s the double-edged sword: coal burns because it’s full of carbon. Burning it gives us energy and puts that carbon straight back into the air.

06 Today

Full circle

So where does our energy come from today? Still mostly from buried sunshine: coal, oil and gas. Only around one-sixth of the world’s energy comes from low-carbon sources like nuclear, hydro, wind and solar, although that share is growing fast, especially in electricity.

But zoom out and there’s a beautiful pattern. For almost all of history we ran on today’s sunshine: food, wood, wind and water. For two centuries we borrowed ancient sunshine from underground. It gave us the modern world, along with a carbon bill. Now we’re learning to run on today’s sunshine again, this time with silicon and steel instead of firewood.

Ancient sunshinecoal, oil, gas

Not to scale. If it were, the first block would be about a million years long and the second barely 200.

In the first half of 2025, for the first time ever, renewables generated more of the world’s electricity than coal. The same sun that powered those first microbes is powering us again. This time, the carbon stays in the ground.

Next time you flick a switch, charge your phone or put the kettle on, ask yourself: where did this energy come from, and what impact does it cause?!
